# Facilitation procedure

**WP-01 v1 · Facilitator-only.** Do not distribute this guide as a learner handout.

## Before the session

Select one cohort of engineers who review consequential software actions. Participation is for improving these materials, with no ranking, certification, or employment decision. Explain what written responses will be retained, who can inspect them, and the facilitator's chosen retention/deletion date. Record only information needed for the pilot. Give reflection time and allow written responses; do not assess fluency, confidence, speed, or willingness to debate.

Assign an instructional editor, capability/source reviewer, visual/accessibility reviewer, adversarial reviewer, and facilitator. Production roles may overlap. Calibration needs 2–3 distinct people giving independent initial judgments. An AI-generated self-check is not an independent human review.

Privately ask each learner about access to the source, strategy, pilot folder, any cards, worked examples, solutions, or discussion of similar cases. Record case-level exposure as known unseen, exposed, or uncertain with its basis. Self-report is a limitation, not a guarantee. Use an unseen substitute when necessary; uncertain or exposed responses remain useful practice but cannot establish an independent baseline or unfamiliar transfer. If no unseen case remains, record that measurement as unavailable.

Prepare individual cards without file names, case IDs, lesson labels, or answers. Number response slots neutrally. Freeze the prompt set and anchors before beginning. The three primary transfer cards must remain unseen until delivery.

## Delivery order

1. Collect all three diagnostics **before any teaching**. This prevents one lesson from supplying another baseline. A and B each have one prompt. For C collect the initial answer, then reveal its separate critic card and collect a second answer. Never overwrite the first answer.
2. Teach the three distinct worked examples using their scripts. Show the situation, ask for a decision, and wait before revealing explanation. Learners can retain a defensible position, correct it, leave it open, or reject formalization. Save worked-example reasoning as practice, separate from assessment.
3. Issue the three transfer cards after teaching, one at a time, with no hints, worked solutions, reference page, or lesson names. Preserve original answers. Record any help, peer discussion, or accidental exposure and its impact on qualification. In C, collect the initial position before the critic card, then the response to both objections.
4. Only after all these responses are frozen, give plain-language feedback and the [reference page](learner-reference.md). Introduce the proposed lens names and technical crosswalk at this point. Record this exposure.
5. Arrange a later session at an interval feasible for the cohort; record the actual elapsed time. Issue unseen L1 cards without labels or hints. They are limited retention/transfer observations, not proof of durable judgment. If exposed, author and review a fresh case or record unavailable.

These are proposed timings: opening and exposure check 5–10 minutes, diagnostics 10–15, teaching 15–20, transfer 10–15, debrief as needed. Extend or split the session rather than rushing. Timing is for production planning, not scoring.

## Neutral learner introduction

“These are fictional situations. We are trying out the questions and explanations. Write what you think and why. You may disagree, change your mind, or say that the information is insufficient if you explain what is missing. You do not need special terms or a diagram. We are not grading your speed or confidence. Tell us if you have seen a question or its solution before.”

## During independent responses

Use only: “Please explain your reason” or repeat the original wording. Record even these interventions. Do not ask leading questions such as “Does that prove everyone can use it?” during assessment. Feedback comes later. If a prompt is ambiguous, record the learner's interpretation; a revision creates a different prompt version. Do not silently add facts to rescue an item.

Initial prompts and feedback use ordinary language. Avoid “admission,” “testimony,” named lenses, or branded diagrams. Professor Voss is unnecessary for this pilot; use an unnamed critic. If a later adaptation adds him, identify him as a fictional composite on first appearance in each asset.

## Calibration and reporting

Use the same 10–20 de-identified responses spanning the lessons for every reviewer, with frozen facts and anchors. Record unique learner count and repeated responses. C's initial/challenge answers form one linked response unit; do not count them as independent learners or duplicate transfer credit.

Give reviewers randomized response IDs with prompt facts but conceal identity, teaching stage, other judgments, and exposure qualification where practicable. The facilitator retains the crosswalk needed to interpret results. Prompt content can reveal stage; document that blinding limit. Each reviewer cites response words and case facts for every elicited dimension. Preserve all first judgments before discussion.

Compare raw exact-agreement counts and denominators by dimension. Include category disagreements and differences over whether a dimension was elicited. Do not average ratings or manufacture an overall score. Discuss prompt ambiguity, anchor ambiguity, legitimate value disagreement, and reviewer preference separately. Revise with a dated reason; test revised anchors on fresh responses. Discussion agreement on the old set is not new validation.

The instructional editor and an independent reviewer complete the findings report, including failures and counterexamples. Learner recruitment, collection, calibration, and findings cannot be replaced by simulated responses or editorial review.
