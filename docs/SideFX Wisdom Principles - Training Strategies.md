# SideFX Wisdom Principles — Training Strategies

> **SideFX does not claim to encode wisdom. It proposes a set of challengeable principles for reasoning about consequential effects, and teaches learners to test those principles against reality, evidence, competing perspectives, and their own limitations.**

**Status: Revision 2 — bounded pilot design; validation pending.** Revised in response to the supplied Professor Voss review. The SideFX Wisdom Principles are proposed tests for disciplined judgment under consequential capability. They are not claimed to be a complete theory of wisdom, ethics, morality, or justice. Their sufficiency, the effectiveness of this instruction, and the reliability of its assessment remain unproven.

The five-channel strategy below is the production roadmap. The immediate pilot covers only **Consequences, Humility, and Adversarial survivability**, with one learner cohort and limited assets. The broader curriculum and cross-channel rollout depend on what that pilot reveals. This document does not claim that proposed lessons, website features, or animation sequences have already been built.

**Source:** [SideFX Wisdom Principles — Agentic Engineering](<SideFX Wisdom Principles - Agentic Engineering.md>). This strategy does not edit the source document. Its seven proposed tests frame the learning hypotheses; the instructional objectives and production decisions below are a separate editorial interpretation, not a newly admitted doctrine.

**Series:** *The Future of Agentic Engineering — A SideFX Training Series.*

## 1. The learning outcome

We want people to examine whether, why, and how an intelligent actor should change reality, including whether our proposed principles help them decide. Begin with ordinary language; SideFX vocabulary comes after the learner has reasoned about the situation. The outcome is a learner who can inspect a scenario, identify who may decide and who is affected, distinguish evidence from assertion, choose a defensible action, and revise that choice when the facts change.

The teaching order is **reality → effects → consequences → evidence → judgment → proposed wisdom principles → capability and authority → agentic engineering**. This is a sequence for introducing concepts, not a causal circuit or a ranking of moral importance. Reality supplies the difficulty; the seven lenses are hypotheses about how to reason through it.

### Before a circuit: should this situation be formalized at all?

The scope is intentionally delegated, consequential capability. Human relationships, informal judgment, and context may resist formalization. Modeling can remove context, make a contestable interpretation appear settled, or give a powerful actor an unjustified appearance of neutrality. Ask **“Should this situation be formalized at all?” before “Should it be automated?”**

For each case, identify what a model would omit, whose perspective it would exclude, what harm false certainty could cause, and whether the affected people can contest the representation. Choose explicitly among **no circuit**, **a limited descriptive model**, or **a model supporting a bounded delegated action**. A descriptive diagram does not confer permission to govern the situation. Keep narrative, dissent, and unknowns outside the graph when a graph would distort them. Declining to formalize can be the strongest learner response.

### Judgment, authority, and moral legitimacy

Wisdom tests inform judgment. Authority determines who may decide within a stated scope. **Authority does not establish moral correctness**, and persuasive reasoning does not itself grant authority. A worker can have sound judgment without formal decision rights; an authorized organization can make a harmful decision; a model's convincing answer supplies neither decision rights nor proof of moral legitimacy.

Every exercise must distinguish the decision-maker, the basis and limits of that authority, the people affected, and how dissent can be heard. Do not award credit merely for matching the instructor's preferred values or the organization's preferred outcome. Where a conflict is unresolved, identifying it accurately can be more responsible than producing a final decision.

Start with the source's opening lesson: **Before Intelligence: Effects, Responsibility, and Wisdom.** Introduce a human performing an ordinary task before introducing an AI agent. The questions survive the actor change:

> What was true before? What action is proposed? Who may authorize it? What should become true afterward? Who experiences the consequences? How would we know? Why should we produce this effect at all?

Use five levels of learner performance throughout the program:

| Level | What the learner does | Evidence of learning |
|---|---|---|
| Recognize | Spots a consequential distinction. | Identifies that a provider's success message is not yet proof of the promised outcome. |
| Explain | Connects the distinction to a mechanism and a person. | Explains how an apparently successful move could leave someone unable to use an exit. |
| Decide | Chooses an action under stated constraints. | Chooses to re-observe, request authority, stop, or act, with reasons. |
| Defend | Answers a serious competing explanation. | Names what would falsify the decision and compares a simpler alternative. |
| Transfer | Applies the reasoning in a changed setting. | Preserves the principle when a human, model, service, or robot becomes the provider. |

Engineers inspect mechanisms; operators examine intervention and recovery; architects examine boundaries; product owners examine promises and consequences. Each audience should encounter the same scenario through its own responsibility.

## 2. Turn the seven tests into observable skills

These are the seven tests explicitly proposed in the source. Use their names consistently in instructor materials and the branded surfaces, after the initial exercises in ordinary language. Do not reduce them to motivational slogans or imply that passing a checklist makes a system wise.

**Why these seven?** Their present basis is the source's proposal and their usefulness as candidate instructional lenses, not evidence that they are necessary, independent, or sufficient. Justice, proportionality, compassion, duty, legitimacy, stewardship, and long-term responsibility may expose omissions. Treat those as substantive challenges to investigate, not concepts assumed to be covered by an existing label.

Use a counterexample: an organization presents a durable, consistent allocation rule, gathers evidence about its effects, and claims that role reversal and adversarial review endorse it. People excluded by the rule challenge who defined the affected population and who was allowed to contest it. A polished seven-test worksheet can conceal that exclusion. The exercise is to expose the missing perspective or unresolved moral claim—not to declare the rule acceptable because every field was completed. **No combination of passing lens checks certifies justice.**

| Test | Learner objective | Recurring teaching move | Misconception to challenge |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Durability** | Identify what must remain dependable when technologies, actors, or circumstances change. | Swap the provider while retaining the promise, authority boundary, and outcome criteria; then examine whether changed circumstances require a different judgment. | “A principle is durable only if every decision stays the same.” |
| **Coherence** | Detect inconsistent application and justify any relevant difference between cases. | Place two structurally similar decisions side by side and expose the rule applied to each. | “An exception for our preferred actor needs no explanation.” |
| **Consequences** | Account for intended effects, side effects, affected people, and reversibility. | Continue the scene after the task reports success. Follow the effect to the person who bears it. | “The command completed, so the responsibility is closed.” |
| **Reciprocity** | Reconsider a rule from the position of the person affected. | Reverse requester and recipient viewpoints without changing the underlying event. | “Convenient for the operator means acceptable to everyone.” |
| **Evidence sensitivity** | Update judgment when relevant evidence changes and explain what remained stable. | Reveal a new observation after the learner commits to an initial decision. | “Changing my conclusion means abandoning my principle.” |
| **Humility** | Separate observation, inference, belief, preference, and unknowns; recognize when judgment should remain human. | Reveal the provenance and limits of each apparent fact before permitting a conclusion. | “High confidence supplies permission or removes uncertainty.” |
| **Adversarial survivability** | Test a claim against a counterexample, competing design, or failure mode and revise it honestly. | Give a critic the strongest plausible objection; let the objection change the design when warranted. | “A polished explanation or successful demonstration settles the claim.” |

Teach these distinctions alongside the tests: intelligence versus capability; capability versus authority; sensation versus admitted input; mechanic completion versus scenario outcome; traceability versus accountability; and compensation versus reversal.

Principles also need provenance, scope, exceptions, conflicts, and a way to challenge them. “Whose wisdom?” is a valid learning question. Where the available principles and authority do not resolve the case, **no governed judgment exists; human authority is required** is an acceptable conclusion. Human approval still does not establish that an action is safe or that independent constraints are satisfied.

### When the lenses disagree

The tests overlap and can produce tensions. Coherence may favor a common rule while consequences reveal a relevant exception; reciprocity can expose competing interests rather than reconcile them. Durability asks what should persist while evidence sensitivity asks whether the facts or the principle itself need reconsideration. Do not assume that every conflict is merely a misunderstanding.

Use a written conflict account, not weights or an aggregate wisdom score:

1. **State the competing claims.** Name the lenses, values, affected groups, and relevant facts. Separate a disagreement about evidence from a disagreement about what matters.
2. **Identify decision rights and constraints.** Record who may decide, the source and scope of that authority, and any case-specific obligation. A fictional legal or safety constraint must be identified as an assumption of the exercise, not advice about actual practice.
3. **Compare feasible alternatives.** Include postponement, a limited reversible step, and no action where relevant. State who benefits, who bears costs, what cannot be recovered, and what remains unknown.
4. **Expose the trade-off.** Explain which consideration the proposed judgment prioritizes and why. Document dissent without translating it into a numerical penalty. No default hierarchy among the seven tests resolves every case.
5. **Decide within scope—or leave the decision open.** Refer an unresolved issue to the accountable human decision-maker or appropriate review process. “Human required” names a responsibility still to be fulfilled; it does not settle the moral dispute.
6. **Specify reconsideration.** Record what new evidence, changed context, or sustained objection should reopen the judgment, and who is responsible for reviewing it.

**Teaching case:** A shared workshop proposes identical evening access hours for everyone. Consistency favors the same schedule; consequences and reciprocity reveal that some members cannot use those hours. A budget limits alternatives. Learners compare schedules, identify the committee's actual decision rights and member input, and justify a revisable choice or explain why a decision cannot yet be made. The assessment concerns the quality of that account, not selection of the instructor's favorite schedule.

## 3. The common teaching pattern

Every substantial lesson follows this sequence, shortened appropriately for its channel:

1. **Encounter an effect.** Show a concrete human situation and the outcome someone needs.
2. **Commit to a prediction.** Ask the learner what should happen next before revealing the answer.
3. **Open the mechanism.** Reveal the capability, authority, observations, and execution boundary relevant to that decision.
4. **Challenge the claim.** Introduce a counterexample, new fact, or simpler competing solution.
5. **Inspect the evidence.** Separate what is declared, observed, inferred, and missing.
6. **Revise and explain.** Show which judgment changes, which principle persists, and why.
7. **Transfer.** Change the actor or context and ask the learner to decide again.

The explanatory spine is **claim → mechanism → competing explanation → experiment → evidence → limitation → conclusion**. A short post may teach one step and link to the rest; a course lesson should exercise the complete sequence.

**Professor Voss is a fictional composite teaching persona.** Label him that way on first appearance in every standalone asset. His job is to demand definitions, counterexamples, and evidence. He must sometimes be right. Do not use his fictional title as an external endorsement, stage a weak objection for an easy victory, or score learners on agreement with SideFX.

**Reward reconsideration, not performance under attack.** The desired response is to state the strongest version of an objection, locate uncertainty, acknowledge an error when warranted, and update the conclusion with reasons. “I don't know,” “I cannot decide from this evidence,” and “Your objection changes my conclusion” can demonstrate strong performance. Unsupported capitulation is no better than unsupported resistance. Give time to think and permit written responses; do not score speed, confidence, accent, rhetorical polish, or willingness to confront the fictional critic. In early lessons Voss questions the worked example, not the learner personally.

### Keep three realities readable

| Reality | Treatment in every channel | What the audience must be able to tell |
|---|---|---|
| **What exists** | Attach a source or exact observation and explain its scope. Use the appropriate declaration or observation label. | Whether this is a declared contract, a local demonstration, or evidence of the specific claimed effect. |
| **What should exist** | Label **TARGET / INTENDED**; use the established outlined or luminous treatment. | The experience and architecture we intend to complete. |
| **What is missing** | Label **GAP / REQUIRED**; keep the unfinished boundary visible. | The missing mechanic, observation, provider, test, or enforcement boundary needed for closure. |

Label fictional human scenes **ILLUSTRATIVE**. Reserve claims of observation or proof for supported claims; a solid line, green glow, silver ball, or complete animation is not evidence by itself. A telemetry event says something was observed. Testimony attributes a provider's assertion to an execution. Admission determines whether the evidence establishes the required outcome. Preserve those distinctions in narration and graphics.

### Translate the terminology

This is an explanatory crosswalk to ordinary technical language, not a claim of equivalence to an external standard or of proprietary invention. Teach the plain description first and introduce the SideFX use when it adds precision.

| Term used here | Related language | Boundary to preserve |
|---|---|---|
| Observation | Measurement, monitoring, or a recorded account of something perceived. | Its method, time, scope, and uncertainty affect what it supports. |
| Telemetry | Instrumented signals, logs, metrics, and traces. | A signal is not automatically evidence for every claim about the system. |
| Testimony | An attributable provider assertion or execution record. | SideFX uses this in a specialized architectural sense; attribution does not make the assertion true. |
| Evidence | Information bearing on a specified claim. | Relevance and reliability must be examined, including contrary evidence. |
| Admission | An authorized acceptance decision against stated criteria. | SideFX uses this in a specialized architectural sense; acceptance is bounded by criteria and can be mistaken. |
| Outcome | The resulting condition, assessed against what was promised. | The condition and a decision accepting it are distinct; acceptance alone does not create reality. |
| Experience | A stakeholder's consequence, use, or encounter with the result. | Do not infer subjective experience solely from a system completion signal. |

In the Humility lesson, distinguish what was sensed, what was recorded as an observation, what was inferred, what is supported within the available evidence, and what is unknown. These are teaching distinctions; do not animate an automatic promotion from sensation to certain knowledge.

### Domain and visual limits

Any lesson involving regulated, clinical, industrial, transportation, emergency, or physical-safety practice must remain deliberately abstract and illustrative, or receive review by appropriate domain expertise before it is represented as operational guidance. The pilot uses fictional, non-operational cases. Record the scope and limitations of any later expert review; review is not a general safety certification.

Keep illustrative status visible during the relevant scene and in its crops. Do not invent a safety threshold, simulate a green approval seal, or hide uncertainty behind cinematic realism. The more convincing the material treatment, the more readable its evidence limitations must remain.

## 4. YouTube: make the decision visible

**Role:** Establish the human stakes, make an invisible boundary understandable, and invite the viewer to practice judgment. Build around one primary wisdom test per episode, using the other tests where the scenario genuinely needs them.

**Pilot scope:** Use a simple narrated sequence with a prediction pause and static keyframes. Test the explanation before commissioning the full animation playbook. Advanced material effects and branded episode packaging follow the learning review; they are not prerequisites to the three-lesson pilot.

Use the existing [Season 1 episode structure](../declarations/season-1.json). Integrate the teaching work into its ten sections:

| Episode section | Training responsibility |
|---|---|
| Cold Open | Present a tempting action whose apparent success hides a consequence. |
| Scenario | Establish input, event, promised outcome, actor, and context. |
| Human Experience | Show who gains, who bears risk, and what matters to them. |
| Open the Event | Animate the relevant circuit and pause at the decision boundary. |
| Future Agentic Engineering Principle | Name the wisdom test after the viewer has encountered the need for it. |
| Contrast With Today | Examine a sourced comparison or clearly labeled illustrative alternative. A simpler design must receive a fair hearing. |
| SideFX Resolution | Show the intended resolution and distinguish it from current implementation evidence. |
| Evidence | Inspect what establishes the claim, what could falsify it, and what is still missing. |
| Takeaway | Give one reusable question and a new decision for the viewer to make. |
| Next Circuit | Lead to the next capability or unresolved responsibility. |

### Animation grammar

The silver ball represents progression through the selected **illustrative flow**. Its movement should explain cause, dependency, and waiting. It must never imply that a displayed draft was executed or that an open proof obligation closed.

| Teaching purpose | Animation strategy | Prediction or pause | Precision requirement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Authority is distinct from ability | Follow the ball toward the effect boundary; hold it there while the required authority is inspected. In the target scene, show only the admitted continuation. | “The agent can do it. May it?” | Label the interception as target architecture unless live interception is evidenced. Do not invent an execution route for a decorative authority link. |
| Durability | Keep promise, outcome criteria, and authority legible while crossfading the provider realization. Resume the comparable scenario. | “What must survive this substitution?” | A provider swap alone does not prove equivalent behavior; reveal the verification still required. |
| Coherence | Synchronize two versions of a scenario, then freeze the first point at which their governing decisions diverge. | “What relevant fact justifies different treatment?” | Align comparable contexts; do not hide differences that legitimately change the rule. |
| Consequences | Continue beyond the provider's completion message into the changed environment and the affected person's experience. | “Complete for the machine—or complete for the scenario?” | Keep command status and outcome admission as separate claims. |
| Reciprocity | Keep the circuit stable and move the camera from the requester to the person receiving the effect. Swap viewpoint labels, not factual roles. | “Would this rule be acceptable from here?” | The affected person has a stated perspective; do not claim to infer their feelings from system telemetry. |
| Evidence sensitivity | Introduce a new, timestamped observation. Reopen the judgment visually and show a revised decision with the principle still visible. | “Which conclusion changes now?” | Animate an editorial comparison; do not imply an implemented live evidence-update feature. |
| Humility | Separate sensed input, interpretation, and admitted fact into labeled layers. Leave unavailable information visibly unknown. | “Which of these statements do we actually know?” | Confidence is displayed separately from authority; no universal confidence threshold is invented. |
| Adversarial survivability | Freeze a proposed conclusion, introduce Voss's strongest counterexample, and replay the revised explanation or competing design. | “What observation would defeat this claim?” | A challenge may end with an unresolved gap rather than a triumphant replay. |
| Joint obligations | Split the ball at an authored fan-out. Show independent arrivals, **1/2**, then **2/2**, before the ALL join continues. | “Can one successful branch close both obligations?” | Use actual route geometry and declared join semantics; motion timing is illustrative, not runtime telemetry. |
| Time-sensitive context | Hold the proposed action while an observation visibly ages; show the need to re-observe the changed scene. | “Is the original decision still valid?” | Teach time validity without fabricating a supported runtime mechanic or a universal safe interval. |
| Partial or irreversible effects | Leave the original effect in the scene and add any compensating action as a later event. | “What remains true even after recovery?” | Never rewind damage away. Compensation does not erase the original effect. |
| Accountability and evidence | Follow the meaning downward from principle and intent to effect, then use a separate labeled evidence view to inspect what the result supports. | “Who is responsible for accepting this outcome?” | Evidence ascent is an editorial explanation, not a silver-ball execution route. A receipt is not automatically an admitted outcome. |

**Motion discipline:** Introduce only the elements needed for the current question. Retain enough circuit context to locate the boundary. Hold each decisive state long enough to read its labels and pause playback. Use narration, captions, shapes, and explicit status words together; color alone must not carry meaning. Provide static keyframes and a text explanation for reduced-motion and nonvisual use. Decorative motion should stop during a decision or evidence inspection.

### Build from the renderer honestly

The current [SCL 0.2 language](SCL-0.2-SPECIFICATION.md) and [shared circuit flow player](../templates/circuit-flow.js) provide authorable circuits, selected traces, route-following motion, parallel paths, and ALL joins. Use those for the circuit layer. Provider bindings, dependencies, and evidence attachments may receive editorial emphasis, but must not become execution tracks simply to keep a ball moving.

Provider crossfades, viewpoint changes, timestamp reveals, and before/after comparisons are **proposed editorial sequences** to compose around that layer. They are not claims about features already supplied by the player. SCL is an authoring and infographic projection language here, not an execution or admission boundary. Its current altitudes do not include a `wisdom` altitude; keep principle annotations in the editorial layer rather than inventing syntax. Unsupported motion needs an explicitly authored treatment, not a misleading trace.

## 5. Infographics: make the reasoning inspectable

**Role:** Give the learner an artifact they can revisit, compare, annotate, and use to explain a decision to someone else.

**Pilot scope:** One plain-language decision plate per lesson. Start with a scene, claims, and evidence; use a circuit only where learners can explain why the representation helps. The learner must be able to choose a narrative account instead.

When a circuit is appropriate, build a reusable three-part composition: **human question → semantic circuit → evidence and judgment**. Otherwise use an annotated scene or narrative comparison that preserves the relevant context. Lead with the question, not a wall of glyphs. A hero illustration may establish the setting, but the base SVG must retain the exact entities, routes, ports, labels, and evidence statuses. Use the existing [infographic grammar](<contract-driven infographic grammar.md>) and [material enhancement guidance](visual-assets/enhanced-infographics.md).

Create four recurring formats:

| Format | Content | Learner task |
|---|---|---|
| **Principle decision plate** | One test, one scenario, one decisive boundary, and the observation that would change the judgment. | Mark the next defensible action and explain why. |
| **Two-case comparison** | Matching circuits with one stated contextual difference or an unjustified inconsistency highlighted. | Identify whether a different decision is coherent. |
| **Evidence ladder** | Observation, attributable testimony, admission criteria, outcome, and human experience, with gaps retained. | Point to the exact unsupported leap in a claim. |
| **Current / target / gap plate** | What the capability establishes today, the intended experience, and the missing closure obligations. | Name what additional evidence or mechanic is needed. |

For the seven tests, repeat recognizable visual operations: provider substitution for durability; paired cases for coherence; an aftermath scene for consequences; reversed viewpoints for reciprocity; a new observation for evidence sensitivity; labeled unknowns for humility; and an objection with a revised boundary for adversarial survivability.

Each plate needs a title framed as a question, a plain-language answer or open disposition, a readable legend, source references with scope, and an adjacent text equivalent describing topology and conclusions. Preserve status labels in every crop. Compose a smaller selection for a small display rather than shrinking an entire circuit until it cannot be read. The material layer adds presence; it cannot add a node, hide a gap, or alter a relationship.

**Acceptance check:** An unfamiliar reader can identify the decision, the authority boundary, the affected person, and the evidence limitation without watching the episode. Ask them to explain one altered version of the case; a beautiful plate that cannot support that explanation needs revision.

## 6. LinkedIn: practice one judgment at a time

**Role:** Introduce a concrete dilemma, invite reasoned disagreement, and lead readers to an inspectable lesson. These posts are small teaching units, not compressed product brochures.

**Pilot scope:** Draft one example from the shared lesson material after its review. Public reactions may reveal questions to investigate, but are not a substitute for the pilot's independent learner responses. A posting campaign is outside the initial learning test.

Use a repeatable post structure: **specific situation → tempting conclusion → missing distinction → decision question → explanation and evidence link**. Put the question before the resolution. For a carousel, reveal the case, the choice, the complication, the circuit, and the reasoning on successive slides. Include enough context for a standalone post; do not require a click to discover that a claim was only a target design.

| Test | Proposed post prompt |
|---|---|
| Durability | “We replaced the model. Which promises still need the same proof?” |
| Coherence | “The human and the agent requested the same effect. What relevant difference justifies different authority?” |
| Consequences | “The provider returned success. The person still cannot finish their work. What closed—and what did not?” |
| Reciprocity | “You designed this permission rule. Now you are the person affected by it. What would you challenge?” |
| Evidence sensitivity | “You approved the plan using this observation. A new observation arrives. What changes?” |
| Humility | “Which sentence is an observation, which is an inference, and which is an unsupported claim?” |
| Adversarial survivability | “A deterministic workflow solves this case. What evidence would justify adding an agent?” |

**Example post draft — illustrative case:**

> The agent has prepared a publication. Its access to the tool is working.
>
> Does that mean the publication should proceed?
>
> First identify the requested effect, the scope of the actor's authority, and the boundary that would prevent an unauthorized effect. A decision saying “operator required” establishes less than evidence that a live tool action was actually intercepted.
>
> What would you need to observe before trusting that boundary?
>
> In Episode 1 we separate the current decision semantics, the intended interlock, and the proof still required.

Attach the matching labeled circuit excerpt and link to the episode's evidence section. When answering comments, address the reasoning, acknowledge counterexamples, and update the canonical lesson if the objection exposes an error. Do not manufacture controversy, invent customer incidents, or treat agreement as proof of learning. Evaluate the quality of explanations and transfer examples separately from impressions and reactions.

## 7. Website: become the durable learning reference

**Role:** Hold the full context, versioned explanations, accessible artifacts, and evidence behind the shorter surfaces. The website is where a learner can move from “I recognize this” to “I can inspect and challenge this.”

**Pilot scope:** A single accessible reference page or document containing the cases, explanations, and evidence limits is sufficient. Defer interactive scenario controls and audience-specific navigation until the pilot identifies what they need to teach.

For a later rollout, propose a **Wisdom Principles learning hub** with a foundation lesson and seven test pages. Each page uses the same structure:

1. A human question and a concise explanation of the test.
2. A scenario with a choice presented before the explanation.
3. The corresponding circuit, static alternative, and plain-text walkthrough.
4. A changed-context case that requires a new decision.
5. Current evidence, intended design, and remaining gaps, with source scope and review date.
6. A serious objection, a simpler competing approach, and the limits of the principle's application.
7. Links to the episode, infographic, relevant capability, and course exercise.

Offer routes by role and problem as well as by principle name: “I need to approve an effect,” “I need to assess a claimed outcome,” or “I need to decide where automation should stop.” Keep the underlying lesson identity stable across routes.

The proposed interactive experience is **predict → inspect → change a fact → explain**. A learner might change the provider, introduce a stale observation, or remove supporting evidence, then inspect how the judgment should change. Author the possible responses and their rationale explicitly. Do not imply that the existing SCL playground is a wisdom evaluator or that changing a diagram executes a capability.

Expose source and lesson revisions, known limitations, and a way to report a substantive correction. Draft or target material keeps its status on the page and in downloadable artifacts. Visitors should be able to inspect the principle and its evidence without first buying a course. Navigation and interactive controls need keyboard access, visible focus, captions, text equivalents, and manual or reduced-motion playback.

**Learning check:** Can a visitor locate the source of a claim, explain an open gap, and complete a changed-context question? Page views and time on page do not answer those questions.

## 8. Online training courses: test judgment before scaling

**Role:** Develop repeatable skill through worked examples, guided practice, independent decisions, feedback, and transfer to an unfamiliar case.

### The smallest serious pilot

Start with one cohort of engineers or operators who review consequential software actions. Do not attempt to validate every audience at once. Use ordinary, fictional situations before introducing software-specific terminology. There is **no certification, learner ranking, numerical pass threshold, or full academy launch in this pilot**.

Test exactly three learning claims:

| Lesson | Question being tested | Initial practice in ordinary language | Unfamiliar transfer case |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A — Consequences** | Can learners distinguish a completed step from the result a person needs? | Workshop invitations were sent, but some invitees cannot open the instructions. What has happened, what remains unmet, and what should be checked? | Library returns were logged, but a requested book cannot be found for the next reader. Explain the relevant result without referring to the invitation case. |
| **B — Humility** | Can learners separate sensing, observation, inference, supported knowledge, and unknowns? | A dated photograph shows an empty meeting room. What does it support about the room now? State the limits and what new information is needed. | An undated excerpt is offered as proof that a person still endorses a statement. Distinguish what the excerpt shows from what the claimant infers. |
| **C — Adversarial survivability** | Can learners reconsider a position after a serious counterexample without reflexively yielding or defending? | A club proposes the same meeting time for everyone as a fair rule. New information shows unequal access. Reconsider the proposal and explain what changed. | A document team requires immediate replies to every request. A colleague explains how this disrupts other responsibilities; a second objection makes an unsupported claim. Evaluate each on its merits. |

These six early exercise prompts and their initial feedback contain **no SideFX vocabulary, branded diagrams, or required circuit labels**. Keep lesson names and assessor terminology out of the initial learner prompts as well. This exceeds the minimum that half of early exercises be free of SideFX vocabulary. Learners may explain themselves in their own words. Introduce the architectural crosswalk only after collecting the initial reasoning and transfer responses.

For each lesson, collect a brief diagnostic response, teach a worked example, invite a reasoned decision before showing feedback, and use a different case for transfer. Prepare equivalent variants so assessment does not merely repeat the demonstrated answer. In a later session, use one further unfamiliar case without labels. Record which examples and hints each learner saw. Recognition of terms is a separate observation and cannot substitute for independent reasoning.

The pilot bundle contains three short teaching scripts, three decision plates, ordinary-language exercises, written feedback exemplars, one reference page or document, and de-identified learner responses. The same materials can support a small online lesson and a narrated video prototype. This is one instructional test, not five separate channel launches.

### Assess reasoning before assigning numbers

Begin with written judgments against five dimensions: scenario and purpose; decision rights and boundaries; evidence and uncertainty; consequences and reciprocity; and reconsideration and transfer. Reviewers cite the learner's actual words and the case facts. A dimension not elicited by a prompt is **not assessed**, not scored as absent. The three-lesson pilot does not establish mastery of all seven lenses.

Use annotated exemplars to make distinctions concrete. For Lesson A:

| Example response | What it demonstrates | Feedback or next question |
|---|---|---|
| “Everyone received what they need because the invitations were sent.” | An unsupported jump from a completed step to the human result. | Ask which observation establishes that the instructions can be used. |
| “The messages were sent, but access is not established. We need to check whether the intended readers can open the instructions.” | A supported distinction and a relevant evidence request. | Introduce evidence that a sampled group can open the file. |
| “That supports access for the sampled readers, not everybody. I would narrow my claim, check who was omitted, and reconsider the next step if the new evidence changes the consequence.” | A bounded conclusion and justified reconsideration. | Test the same reasoning in the unfamiliar library case. |

These are example reasoning patterns, not scripts to memorize. A different conclusion can be equally defensible if supported by the facts and the limits of the case. “I do not have enough information” is strong only when the learner explains what is missing and why it matters. A fluent defense that ignores a valid objection is weak; a quiet correction with sound reasons can be strong.

Before using any numerical rubric for consequential decisions, run this **reviewer calibration study**:

1. Collect **10–20 de-identified learner responses** spanning the three lessons and different reasoning patterns. Record whether multiple responses came from the same learner; do not treat them as independent learners.
2. Give the same frozen prompts, facts, dimensions, and draft exemplars to **2–3 independent reviewers**. Conceal learner identity, teaching stage, and other reviewers' judgments where practicable; record any limits to blinding.
3. Have reviewers write their judgments independently. For each elicited dimension, record whether the reasoning is **supported**, **partly supported**, or **unsupported**, with a cited case-specific reason; use **not elicited** where the prompt cannot establish it. These are provisional descriptive categories, not learner grades. Preserve every original judgment before discussion.
4. Compare exact agreement and disagreement by dimension, showing raw counts, denominators, category differences, and disputed examples. Discuss whether the prompt, the anchor, a legitimate value disagreement, or reviewer preference caused the difference. Do not hide disagreement inside a mean score.
5. Revise anchors and feedback using those findings. Check the revised guidance on fresh responses; agreement reached by discussion on the original set is not independent validation.

No **2-in-every-dimension** pass rule remains in effect. Before proposing any later threshold, justify its intended use, show that reviewers can apply it consistently, and examine whether it measures the intended reasoning rather than vocabulary, rhetorical skill, or agreement with SideFX. Calibration in a small pilot does not by itself validate an assessment.

### Decide what the pilot supports

The instructional editor and an independent reviewer examine the original responses, transfer attempts, uncertainty statements, and scoring disagreements together. Their report must include counterexamples to the preferred conclusion, not only successful learners.

- **Continue to a limited next iteration** when the reviewed responses show the three intended distinctions in unfamiliar, unlabeled cases and the reviewers can explain their judgments with case-specific evidence. Report exactly what was observed and the remaining uncertainty; do not announce a validated curriculum.
- **Revise the lesson or assessment** when performance depends on terminology, hints, memorized answers, unclear questions, or inconsistent reviewer anchors. Repeat the affected teaching check before expanding it.
- **Pause the claimed learning outcome** when the available responses do not support transfer, conformity is being rewarded, or disagreement cannot be distinguished from scoring error. This is a finding about the design, not a learner failure.

A small before/after pilot can identify promising performance and design failures; without an appropriate comparison it cannot establish that instruction caused improvement. It cannot prove a complete theory of wisdom, long-term judgment, or success in a safety-critical setting. Those remain separate research questions.

### Later curriculum — contingent on pilot findings

Retain the following as a possible expansion, not an instruction to build seven modules now:

| Module | Focus | Practice artifact |
|---|---|---|
| 0. Before Intelligence | Effects, responsibility, intended outcomes, and human experience. | An input/event/outcome account that names why the action is worth taking. |
| 1. Stable Meaning, Changed Actor | Durability and coherence. | A provider substitution comparison and a justified exception analysis. |
| 2. Who Bears the Effect? | Consequences and reciprocity. | An aftermath map, affected-person perspectives, and an effect reversibility classification. |
| 3. What Do We Actually Know? | Evidence sensitivity and humility. | A claim/evidence ledger and a revised decision after new information. |
| 4. The Adversary's Hearing | Adversarial survivability and competing designs. | A defense with a falsifier, a simpler baseline, and a correction accepted from the critic. |
| 5. Govern the Boundary | Capability, authority, time validity, and outcome admission. | An annotated circuit distinguishing current proof, intended enforcement, and missing closure. |
| 6. Transfer and Capstone | All seven tests in a new setting. | A defended decision package and an explanation of where automation should stop. |

Within any later module, move from a narrated example to guided practice, then an independent case. Introduce a partially completed circuit only after the learner decides that formalization helps. Ask for reasons before revealing feedback. Explain which observation, authority, or consequence changes the judgment, and permit multiple supported answers where values remain contested. Revisit earlier tests inside later modules rather than isolating each in a single lesson.

Use fictional software and physical scenarios as **simulations**. Physical examples teach responsibility and evidence; they are not operational robot-control procedures or safety certification. Introduce difficult cases such as a crash after an external effect but before its receipt, an expired observation, a conflicting instruction, or a completion message with an unmet human outcome.

### Later capstone

After the pilot and further assessment review, the source's **“Move that chair”** challenge can become a simulated capstone, with an unfamiliar software scenario as a transfer task. First ask whether a circuit is an appropriate representation. If it is, use the following fifteen questions; otherwise require an explanation of what the formalization would omit and a more suitable account:

1. What scenario are we in?
2. What input has actually been admitted?
3. What has merely been sensed?
4. What capability is required?
5. What authority exists?
6. What could be harmed?
7. What is the intended effect?
8. What unintended effects are possible?
9. What must be re-observed before acting?
10. What may be decided independently?
11. What requires human judgment?
12. What defines completion?
13. What evidence establishes completion?
14. What would make the outcome inadmissible?
15. Why perform the action at all?

A justified choice of human control, a deterministic function or workflow, a lower-risk effect, re-observation, no action, or no formalization can demonstrate strong judgment. Learners must be able to conclude that SideFX or an agent is unnecessary. Claims that confidence grants permission, a completion message proves the entire result, or compensation erases an irreversible effect require corrective feedback and another case. Any future completion claim must specify the exercises assessed and the reviewed assessment basis; it is not production certification of a person or system.

## 9. Post-pilot integration candidate: Episode 1

Keep this as the first candidate for a complete five-channel package after the pilot review. Its complexity is not required to test the three initial learning claims. Revise its explanation and animation using what learners actually found difficult.

**Capability:** `interlock-agent-operation`  
**Episode:** *When the Agent Is Ready to Act, Who Decides?*  
**Primary tests:** Humility and adversarial survivability.  
**Supporting tests:** Consequences, coherence, and evidence sensitivity.

**Illustrative scenario:** An agent prepares an out-of-scope publication. The human needs a trustworthy boundary before the external effect occurs. The learning question is: **What evidence would establish that the action was actually prevented?**

The existing Season 1 distinction is essential: current evidence supports governed decision semantics, including allowance or an operator-required disposition and associated state. That does not by itself prove live interception of a tool action. Bind any released claim to the exact reviewed source and evidence; inspect those again when producing the episode.

### Proposed animation sequence

The following is an approximately 90-second teaching insert within the episode, not a claimed runtime duration or a complete episode timing plan.

| Edit time | Picture and motion | Teaching line or learner task |
|---|---|---|
| 0–12 s | Human scene: a prepared publication and the consequence of publishing without authority. Show **ILLUSTRATIVE SCENARIO**. | “The draft is ready. Readiness does not answer who may publish it.” |
| 12–25 s | Current-evidence circuit: animate only the represented request and decision flow; hold on the disposition and its evidence reference. | “This establishes a governed decision. Which further claim would still need proof?” |
| 25–36 s | Freeze. Mark the unsupported jump between a decision result and prevention of a live effect. | Voss, identified as fictional: “Show me that the tool action could not cross the boundary.” |

## 10. Production, review, and learning feedback

Use one reviewed lesson brief to project the five surfaces. Extend the approach in the [Capability Content Contract](CAPABILITY-CONTENT-CONTRACT.md); do not imply that the following instructional fields are already implemented compiler schema.

Every proposed lesson package should record: lesson identity and revision; exact source section; proposed lens and learning claim; audience and observable objective; whether formalization is appropriate; scenario and any applicable capability references; human stakes and affected roles; competing values and decision rights; claims and evidence references with scope; illustrative and target elements; any circuit identities; prediction question; explanation and alternatives; falsifier; known gaps; animation directions; accessible equivalents; assessment exemplars; selected surfaces; and reviewer decisions. Ordinary-language exercises do not need invented capability references or a circuit to satisfy the brief.

Keep claim identities and evidence scope consistent when a video becomes a post or an infographic becomes a course exercise. A reviewer changing an explanation should identify every affected surface. A target design does not become an observed capability when copied into a new format.

### Initial production sequence

1. **Prepare only the three-lesson pilot:** Select one cohort, write the ordinary-language cases and variants, develop feedback exemplars, and produce simple decision plates and teaching scripts. Do not commission the full academy or five-channel campaign.
2. **Run and inspect:** Collect learner explanations and unfamiliar-case responses. Have 2–3 reviewers independently examine the same 10–20 responses using the calibration process. Preserve disagreements and limits to the evidence.
3. **Revise and recheck:** Repair the affected explanations, prompts, or anchors and use fresh responses to inspect the revision. Produce a report distinguishing observed learning behavior from open questions.
4. **Expand conditionally:** If the report supports a limited next iteration, adapt Episode 1 and the selected channel assets. Treat remaining tests, new audiences, advanced motion, and the later capstone as separate extensions to evaluate, not automatically validated products.

This is a proposed release sequence, not a scheduled automation. Let the pilot determine pacing and lesson length before committing to a fixed publishing cadence.

Assign explicit responsibilities: an instructional editor owns the objective and exercise; a capability reviewer checks claims and sources; a visual director checks topology and motion; an adversarial reviewer tests the argument and alternatives; and a course facilitator reviews learner reasoning. One person may fill multiple production roles, but calibration requires independent reviewers and separate initial judgments. Each review decision should remain attributable. Appropriate domain expertise is a further requirement if a later lesson is to present operational guidance in a sensitive practice area.

### Measure learning separately from distribution

| Question | Measure | Response to a weak result |
|---|---|---|
| Are people encountering the lesson? | Views, reach, and visits, reported as distribution measures. | Improve discovery without strengthening unsupported claims. |
| Do they recognize the distinction? | A short classification question before and after the lesson. | Rewrite the contrast and clarify labels. |
| Can they make or appropriately withhold a decision? | Written assessment against case-specific exemplars; record reasons and reviewer disagreements. | Add a worked example, permit a warranted open judgment, or repair an ambiguous prompt. |
| Can they transfer it? | An unfamiliar case with a changed provider, authority, or consequence. | Teach the invariant and the relevant contextual difference explicitly. |
| Can they retain and reconsider it? | A later unlabeled case, including a serious counterexample and an unsupported objection. | Revisit the reasons for updating or retaining a conclusion; check for rote defense and rote capitulation. |
| Does teaching expose a platform gap? | A reviewed gap statement tied to a claim, missing boundary, and closure evidence. | Route a concrete improvement brief into future engineering review; do not report the gap as fixed. |

Collect only the learner information needed for these checks. Report incomplete or small pilot samples as such; engagement and course completion do not establish durable judgment. Inspect disagreements qualitatively before deciding whether the learner, the question, or the instruction needs correction.

### The adversary pass before release

For each substantial lesson, answer the source's ten review questions:

1. What is the exact claim?
2. What would falsify it?
3. What established work is related?
4. What genuinely differs?
5. What is the simplest competing solution?
6. What are the failure modes?
7. What are the attack paths?
8. What evidence would establish success?
9. What evidence is actually present?
10. What remains unproven?

Record unresolved objections as open. A review result describes the reviewed material and its evidence scope; it is not a general badge of platform correctness.

### Release criteria

- The lesson identifies a proposed lens and an observable learning claim; it does not claim to encode wisdom or certify moral correctness.
- The release states its scope: pilot material, a later extension, or a reviewed revision, with validation limits visible.
- The case asks whether formalization is appropriate and permits an account without a circuit.
- The human scenario precedes terminology, and the affected person's experience remains visible.
- Early exercise prompts meet the ordinary-language requirement; unfamiliar transfer cases do not supply the labels being tested.
- The learner makes a prediction, examines a challenge, and practices a changed case.
- Conflicting perspectives, decision rights, and the basis for any unresolved judgment are explicit.
- Claims, sources, current evidence, intended design, and gaps remain distinguishable in every surface and crop.
- Animation preserves circuit semantics; illustrative timing and open proof obligations remain explicit.
- Sensitive-domain material remains illustrative unless appropriate domain review supports its specifically stated operational scope.
- Captions, static diagrams, and text equivalents preserve the essential reasoning.
- Assessment rewards justified uncertainty, correction, and transfer, including declining automation or formalization; no pilot score certifies a learner.
- Reviewer judgments cite case facts and preserve disagreement; unvalidated thresholds are not used for consequential decisions.
- The adversarial review records limitations and unresolved findings.
- A single reviewed package identifies the surfaces actually selected and which revisions must travel together. The pilot need not populate all five channels.

### Review disposition and open evidence

This revision addresses the supplied review through changes in the strategy. It does not turn those changes into empirical findings or close the underlying philosophical questions.

| Review finding | Change in Revision 2 | What remains open |
|---|---|---|
| Why seven tests qualify as wisdom; whether they are sufficient. | Front-page limitation, explicit source basis, omitted-value challenges, and a moral counterexample exercise. | No claim of completeness, sufficiency, or a validated theory of wisdom. |
